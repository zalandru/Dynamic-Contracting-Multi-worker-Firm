\begin{abstract}
%Why do firms layoff workers instead of cutting their wages? I propose that firms optimally layoff less productive workers and then choose not to cut wages for the survivors. Using French matched employer-employee data, I document that the connection between rigid wages and layoffs exists not only across firms, but also within firms, across worker tenure. Standard stories of wage rigidity for exogenous reasons cannot explain this data. I then build a dynamic model of a frictional labor market with on-the-job search, where firms employ workers of heterogeneous match quality and tenure. Firms are not allowed to wage discriminate within a cohort, and thus layoff poor matches. The survivors within the cohort are of higher average quality, so the firm has little incentive to cut their wages. I calibrate the model on the French data and find that the model can replicate the connection between layoffs and rigid wages across both firms and tenure.

Firms rarely cut wages but often lay off workers. Using matched employer–employee data from France, I show that this pattern is not only widespread across firms but also systematic within firms across worker tenure: junior workers experience up to 5 times the layoff risk of senior workers, but suffer little to no wage cuts. Standard explanations of wage rigidity based on exogenous constraints cannot account for this evidence. I develop an equilibrium search model where firms employ risk-averse workers of varying match quality on dynamic contracts. Although theoretically the firm has to manage individual contracts with a continuum of employees, I show that the model can be solved tractably with tenure-specific contracts. Within a cohort, the firm is not allowed to wage discriminate, but can fire freely. This constraint induces firms to shed low-quality matches while keeping surviving matches within the cohort at unchanged wages. Calibrated to the French data, the model replicates the observed link between layoffs and wage rigidity across both firms and tenure, offering a new explanation for why firms fire rather than cut pay.

\emph{Keywords}: productivity, layoffs, long-term contracts \\

\section{Conclusion}
This paper studies the connection between wage cuts and layoffs. I build a model in which firms hire employ workers of various tenure and match quality. Firms use layoffs as a tool to shed poor matches and choose to keep wages of the survivors largely unchanged. The quantitative model accounts well for the patterns of wage pass-through and layoffs documented in the data. 
%I estimate wage cyclicality separately for different jobs, based on their hiring pools. I use the matched employer-employee data to find that wages, both for new and incumbent workers, are significantly more rigid in jobs hiring from unemployment than in jobs poaching workers. I then simulate a model incorporating the separation of search and heterogeneous wage rigidity across different submarkets to find that wage rigidity in the entry-level submarket matters significantly more for the volatility of unemployment than in the higher submarkets. Together, the empirical and the theoretical results imply that, for unemployment volatility purposes, wages are highly rigid. I confirm this by comparing the heterogeneous wage cyclicality calibration of the model to the homogeneous one and find that accounting for the heterogeneity across jobs increases the unemployment volatility by $20\%$.
