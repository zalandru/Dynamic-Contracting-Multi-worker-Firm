\begin{abstract}
This paper proposes a new mechanism for wage rigidity based on firms’ selection of which workers to retain. I develop a model of a frictional labor market with directed search and dynamic contracts in which firms cannot condition wages on match-specific productivity. Instead, firms use layoffs to remove low-productivity matches, improving workforce composition and increasing the value of retained workers. This generates endogenous wage rigidity.
The model implies that workers more exposed to layoffs exhibit more rigid wages. Using French matched employer–employee data, I document this pattern both across firms and within firms across worker tenure. Because workers within a firm face the same shocks, this cross-tenure variation isolates the role of selection. A calibrated version of the model replicates these facts and shows that selection plays a central role in shaping wage dynamics, while exogenous rigidities such as minimum wages have limited effects.

I build an equilibrium search model where firms employ risk-averse workers of heterogeneous match quality on dynamic contracts. Asymmetric information about match quality generates privately inefficient layoffs. After negative productivity shocks, firms fire low-quality matches while smoothing survivors’ wages. The model predicts heterogeneous composition of income risk: recently hired workers face higher layoff risk, whereas senior workers experience larger wage movements. I confirm this pattern using French matched employer–employee data. Finally, because layoffs and wage smoothing are endogenous, exogenous wage constraints have limited additional employment effects: in a calibrated model of France, a 20\% minimum wage increase raises layoffs only modestly and has muted effects on hiring.

I argue instead that it reflects an active decision by firms: layoffs help firms improve workforce composition, making wage cuts less desirable. I develop an equilibrium search model where firms employ risk-averse workers of varying match productivity on dynamic contracts. To facilitate the role of layoffs, I assume that, between workers on the same contract, the firm is not allowed to wage discriminate, but can fire freely. This constraint induces firms to shed low-productivity matches while smoothing wages of the survivors. The model uniquely predicts that this layoff–wage rigidity relationship arises not only across firms but also within firms, across worker tenure. Using matched employer–employee data from France, I document that, consistent with the theoretical prediction, junior workers face up to five times higher layoff risk than senior workers yet experience virtually no wage pass-through. Policy analysis of the quantified model shows that the minimum wage, a source of exogenous wage rigidity that is particularly prevalent in France, has minimal impact on both job-finding and layoff rates.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper proposes a new perspective on the relationship between wage rigidity and layoffs. Rather than viewing rigid wages as a constraint that forces firms to adjust through employment, I show that wage rigidity can arise endogenously from firms’ selection of which workers to retain. When firms face heterogeneous match quality and cannot condition wages on individual productivity, they use layoffs to remove low-productivity matches. This improves workforce composition, raises the value of retained workers, and reduces firms’ incentives to cut wages. As a result, layoffs and wage rigidity are complements rather than substitutes.

Using matched employer–employee data from France, I document that this mechanism is consistent with the joint behavior of wages and layoffs across firms and across worker tenure. Firms that adjust employment more exhibit more rigid wages, and within firms, workers who are more exposed to layoffs—such as recent hires—experience weaker wage pass-through. Because these patterns arise within firms facing the same shocks, they provide direct evidence for the role of selection in shaping wage dynamics.

Quantitative analysis shows that this mechanism is economically important. A calibrated version of the model reproduces the differential response of wages and layoffs across worker groups and highlights the role of selection in shaping the distribution of income risk. The model also provides a new perspective on labor market policies. When firms already have endogenous incentives to smooth wages and use layoffs as a selection device, additional sources of wage rigidity—such as minimum wages—have limited effects on employment dynamics. Similarly, allowing for endogenous severance pay shows that tenure-dependent severance can arise as an optimal risk-sharing arrangement rather than as a constraint on firms’ decisions.

Taken together, these results suggest that wage rigidity cannot be understood solely as the outcome of constraints or insurance motives. Instead, it reflects firms’ optimal responses to heterogeneity within their workforce and their need to decide which workers to retain. More broadly, the paper highlights that understanding wage dynamics requires accounting for how firms jointly adjust wages and employment, and how these decisions interact through endogenous selection.
