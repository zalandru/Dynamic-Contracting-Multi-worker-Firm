\section{Model}
I present a model of a frictional labor market in which firms sign workers of varying match quality to \emph{dynamic} contracts. The model is designed to capture the heterogeneity in wage pass-through and layoffs across firms and tenure documented in the data.

\subsection{Environment} % Measure of workers and firms, production+costs (open+exist?)
Time is discrete and indexed by $t$. The economy is populated by a continuum of firms of measure $1$, indexed by $j \in [0,1]$, and a continuum of workers of measure $I$, indexed by $i \in [0,I]$. Both types of agents are ex ante homogeneous and infinitely lived, with time-separable preferences and discount factor $\beta$. Firms are owned by outside investors who diversify firm-specific productivity risk. Thus firms maximize
\[
E_0 \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \beta^t \pi_{jt}.
\]
Workers are risk-averse and have no access to financial markets. They consume home production $b$ when unemployed and wage $w$ when employed. Their utility is
\[
E_0 \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \beta^t u(c_{it}), \qquad u(c)=\frac{c^{1-\sigma}}{1-\sigma}.
\]

\subsubsection*{Production}
Firms may pay $\kappa_e$ to start producing and must pay $\kappa_f$ each period to remain open. Open firms employ a measure\footnote{The law of large numbers applies and is used throughout (\textcite{Sun2009}).} $n$ of workers. Each worker–firm match is either high or low quality and remains so for the duration of the match. Only the firm observes the quality of an individual match; the proportion of high-quality matches in the firm, $z$, is common knowledge. Production exhibits decreasing returns to scale in size $n$ and, potentially, in quality $z$.\footnote{In the quantitative analysis, I restrict attention to decreasing returns in quality-adjusted quantity $g(z)n$.} Production is also subject to firm-level shocks $y \in \mathcal{Y}$. Output depends on the numbers of high- and low-quality matches, $n_H$ and $n_L$, equivalently on $n$ and $z$:
\[
yF(n_H,n_L)\equiv yF(n,z), \qquad n=n_H+n_L,\quad z=\frac{n_H}{n_H+n_L}.
\]

\subsubsection*{Labor market}  % OJS, directed search, entry, layoffs
Each period, entering firms immediately hire $\tilde{n}=1$ workers; incumbents hire $\tilde{n}\ge 0$. Workers—both employed and unemployed—search for jobs. Matching occurs in a frictional labor market with directed search, as in \textcite{moen1997}. There is a continuum of submarkets indexed by the promised value $v$ to the worker. Firms choose in which submarket to post vacancies at cost $c$, and workers choose where to search. Within each submarket, matches are formed according to a constant-returns-to-scale matching function; market tightness $\theta_v$ suffices to determine matching probabilities. Let $p(\theta_v)$ and $q(\theta_v)\le 1$ denote the job-finding and vacancy-filling probabilities.

Firms are not restricted to a discrete number of vacancies and can deterministically hire $\tilde{n}$ workers from submarket $v$ at cost $\tilde{n}\,c/q(\theta_v)$. The probability that a newly hired match is high quality is $z_0 \in [0,1]$, constant across agents and time. Upon hiring, the firm commits to deliver expected discounted utility $v$. The hiring trade-off is between the cost of hiring, $c/q(\theta_v)$, and the (higher) cost of employing a worker as $v$ rises. Firms can downsize by laying off a fraction $s \in [0,1]$ of their workforce and by incentivizing incumbents to find jobs elsewhere.

I consider two versions of the economy: a steady state and, under an additional assumption (Appendix~\ref{blocrecur}), a block-recursive equilibrium following \textcite{menzio2011} and \textcite{schaal2017}. In both cases, agents need not track the aggregate cross-sectional distribution.

Each period has four stages (Figure~\ref{fig:Timing}). First, production occurs: the firm collects output and pays wage $w$ to each worker. Next, the firm lays off a fraction $s\ge 0$ of its workforce. Fired workers become unemployed and cannot search until next period. Then all workers—employed and unemployed—search, and all firms (entrants and incumbents) hire $\tilde{n}$. Hiring and search choices occur before next-period productivity $y_{t+1}$ is realized, so agents take expectations $E_{y_{t+1}\mid y_t}$.

\subsubsection*{Information structure and contracts}
Upon hiring, the firm commits to deliver expected utility $v$ via a contract. A contract specifies wages and actions for the matched pair for all future firm-productivity histories $y^\tau \equiv (y_1,\ldots,y_\tau)\in \mathcal{Y}^{\tau}$. Firm productivity histories are common knowledge and therefore fully contractible. By contrast, match-specific productivity $z_{ij}$ is private to the firm, and the worker’s search decision $\hat{v}$ is private to the worker. The contract is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{C}=\{w_{\tau},\,s_{\tau},\,\hat{v}_{\tau}\}_{\tau=t}^{\infty}.
\end{equation}
Here $w$ is the wage policy for each future productivity history. The second component, $s$, is the \emph{expected} layoff probability from the worker’s perspective (who does not observe their match quality). These probabilities are history-dependent: in histories where information about match quality is updated, future layoff probabilities reflect the worker’s Bayesian update. For example, after multiple negative shocks that force large layoffs, remaining workers rationally assign higher probability to being high quality, and subsequent layoff probabilities adjust accordingly. The last component is the worker’s (unobserved) search decision. I focus on contracts in which recommended search is incentive compatible, i.e., the contract specifies workers’ search choices subject to the constraint that those choices are optimal for workers.

The contract space allows fully flexible wage and layoff responses to productivity histories. With a continuum of concurrent contracts, the firm can choose how to treat a heterogeneous workforce (by quality and contract): when a negative shock hits, whom to fire and whose wages to cut. This feature is central and specific to the setting: unlike models with CRS production, these decisions depend on the entire firm state; unlike dynamic models with Nash bargaining (\textcite{mccrary2022}) or sequential bargaining (\textcite{bilal2022}), workers within the same firm may optimally face different wages, layoff risks, and responses to shocks.

\subsection{Value functions}
The contract and all agents’ problems admit a recursive formulation. I begin with workers’ problems and then turn to firms managing a continuum of contracts. I show that the firm’s problem can be reformulated with a discrete state space.

\subsubsection*{Worker’s problem} % Value functions, including search trade-off
Unemployed workers consume $b$ and each period choose the submarket that offers the best trade-off between promised future utility and job-finding probability. In a stationary equilibrium (suppressing time subscripts), the value of unemployment $U$ is
\begin{equation}\label{unempproblem}
U=\max_{v}\; u(b)+\beta\big[(1-p(\theta_v))U+p(\theta_v)v\big].
\end{equation}

Consider an employed worker owed value $v$. Suppose the firm pays wage $w$ this period, lays off with probability $s$, and promises future value $v'$ from next period onward. The worker’s search problem is
\begin{equation}\label{empproblem}
v=\max_{\hat{v}}\; u(w)+\beta\Big[sU+(1-s)\big((1-p(\theta_{\hat{v}}))v'+p(\theta_{\hat{v}})\hat{v}\big)\Big].
\end{equation}
The optimal search target $\hat{v}$ depends only on the promised $v'$. By raising $v'$, the firm induces search in higher-$\hat{v}$ submarkets, lowering the probability that the worker exits. Equivalently,
\[
v=u(w)+\beta\big[sU+(1-s)R(v')\big],
\]
where $R(v')\equiv \max_{\hat{v}}\big[(1-p(\theta_{\hat{v}}))v'+p(\theta_{\hat{v}})\hat{v}\big]$ is the worker’s optimal continuation value given promise $v'$ and no layoff.

\subsubsection*{Firm’s problem}
A firm employs a measure $n$ of workers. Let $P(v)$ denote the distribution of promised values owed to incumbents. For each $v$, the firm chooses the wage $w_v$, layoff rate $s_v$, and next period’s productivity-history–contingent promised values $\{v_{v,y'}'\}$. The firm may also hire $\tilde{n}$ workers at value $\tilde{v}$. Each match can be high or low quality. The firm cannot set quality-contingent wages; it affects match quality only through layoffs.\footnote{Appendix~\ref{microfoundation} shows that, under sufficiently inelastic on-the-job search, this is a pooling equilibrium of a signaling game in which the firm \emph{could} set quality-contingent wages.}
The recursive problem is
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
J(y,n,P(v),z)=\max_{\tilde{n},\tilde{v},\,\{w_v,s_v,v'_{v,y'}\}} \;& yF(n,z) - \int w_v\,dP(v) - \tilde{n}\frac{c}{q(\tilde{v})} - \kappa_f + \beta\,E_{y'\mid y}\,J\big(y',n',P'(v)\big) \\
\text{s.t.}\qquad & u(w_v)+\beta\big[s_v U+(1-s_v)R(v'_v)\big]=v \quad \forall v, \\
& v'_v=E_{y'\mid y}\,v'_{v,y'} \quad \forall v, \\
& n'=n\int (1-s_v)\big(1-p(v'_v)\big)\,dP(v)+\tilde{n}, \\
& n'P'(v)=n\int E_{y'\mid y}\,\mathbbm{1}\{v'_{v,y'}\le v\}(1-s_v)\big(1-p(v'_v)\big)\,dP(v)+\mathbbm{1}\{\tilde{v}\le v\}\tilde{n}.
\end{split}
\end{equation*}
The firm maximizes the present value of profits subject to honoring each worker’s promised value. Because search occurs before the next productivity state is realized, workers care about the expected promised value $v'_v$ rather than particular realizations $v'_{v,y'}$. The last two constraints describe the laws of motion for firm size and the distribution of promises.

\paragraph{Discretizing the problem}\label{subsection:discrete}
As written, the state includes a probability distribution—an uncountably infinite-dimensional object. I show that the state space can be discretized, yielding a countably infinite state. First, under directed search, a firm posts in a single submarket and hires at a single value $\tilde{v}$.\footnote{I rule out mixed strategies by an individual firm: if indifferent across submarkets, it posts in one of them.} Hence, all workers hired in the same period by the same firm are owed the same expected utility—both at hiring and, given no quality-contingent wages, thereafter. It is therefore equivalent to work with the CDF $P(v)$ or the PMF $\mathbb{P}(V=v)$, with $P(v)=\sum_{v'\le v}\mathbb{P}(V=v')$. For a firm of age $K<\infty$, there are at most $K$ distinct promised values with positive probability, corresponding to cohorts by tenure $k=t-t_{\text{hired}}\le K$. The state can thus be recast by tenure:

\begin{lemma}\label{firmproblem}
For a firm of age $K$, the problem $J(y,n,P(v))$ is equivalent to
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
J\big(y,\{n_k,v_k,z_k\}_{k\le K}\big)
=\max_{\tilde{n},\tilde{v},\,\{v'_{y',k},w_k,s_k\}_{k\le K}} \;&
yF\!\Big(\sum_k n_k,\;\frac{\sum_k n_k z_k}{\sum_k n_k}\Big)
-\sum_k w_k n_k - \tilde{n}\frac{c}{q(\tilde{v})} - \kappa_f \\
&\qquad + \beta\,E_{y'\mid y}\,J\big(y',\{n'_k,v'_k,z'_k\}_{k\le K+1}\big) \\
\text{s.t.}\qquad
& u(w_k)+\beta\big[s_k U+(1-s_k)R(v'_{k+1})\big]=v_k \quad \forall k\le K, \\
& v'_{k+1}=E_{y'\mid y}\,v'_{k+1,y'} \quad \forall k\le K, \\
& n'_{k+1}=n_k(1-s_k)\big(1-p(v'_{k+1})\big)+\tilde{n} \quad \forall k\le K, \\
& z'_{k+1}=\min\!\Big\{\frac{z_k}{1-s_k},\,1\Big\} \quad \forall k\le K, \\
& n'_0=\tilde{n},\quad v'_0=\tilde{v},\quad z'_0=z_0.
\end{split}
\end{equation*}
\end{lemma}

This representation both discretizes the state space and clarifies how layoffs and wages interact within cohorts. Although workers do not know their own match quality, they know the cohort-level share of high-quality matches. New hires start with $z_0$, and while $z_k$ may evolve, all workers of the same tenure $k$ share the same probability $z_k\ge z_0$ of being high quality. This probability, common knowledge to the firm and workers, depends on layoffs in the cohort. As shown below, average cohort quality has a first-order effect on wage growth, linking wage pass-through and layoffs within the firm.

\subsubsection*{Free entry and exit}
Firms enter by paying $\kappa_e$. Upon entry, a firm draws productivity and starts with a single worker. Free entry pins down expected profits at entry:
\begin{equation}\label{freeentry}
\kappa_e \ge \max_{v_0}\; \Big(-\frac{c}{q(\theta_{v_0})}+\beta\,E_y\,J\big(y,\{1,0,\ldots\},\{v_0,\ldots\},\{z_0,\ldots\}\big)\Big).
\end{equation}
Empirically, this implies a negative relationship between entry costs $\kappa_e$ and vacancy-filling probabilities $q(\theta)$: cheaper entry tightens the labor market, raising job-finding rates and lowering vacancy-filling probabilities.

Incumbents pay an operating cost $\kappa_f$ each period to stay open. With $\kappa_f$ already in $J$, firms remain open if
\begin{equation}\label{freeexit}
J\big(y,\{n_k,v_k,z_k\}\big)\ge 0.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Equilibrium}
An equilibrium is a sequence of policies, matching rates, and distributions of workers and firms across submarkets $v$ such that, each period:
\begin{itemize}
\item Firms solve the problem in Lemma~\ref{firmproblem}.
\item Workers solve \eqref{unempproblem} and \eqref{empproblem}.
\item Free-entry and free-exit conditions \eqref{freeentry}–\eqref{freeexit} hold.
\item Job-finding and vacancy-filling probabilities are consistent with the matching function.
\item Tightness $\theta_v$ is consistent with firms’ posting and workers’ search strategies.
\item The labor market clears.
\end{itemize}
Under the assumption in Appendix~\ref{blocrecur}, the equilibrium may be block recursive—independent of the aggregate distributions. I use that assumption in the quantitative analysis, but not in the theoretical discussion, where I focus on the steady state described above.

\subsection{Mechanism}
I show how the model generates the empirical connection between layoffs and wage pass-through, and the associated tenure heterogeneity.

\paragraph{Wage growth}
\begin{proposition}\label{prop:wagegrowth}
For any current state $(y,\{n_k,v_k,z_k\})$, wages evolve according to
\[
\frac{1}{u'(w'_{k+1})}-\frac{1}{u'(w_k)}
=\eta(v'_{k+1})\,E_{y'\mid y}\,\frac{\partial J\big(y',\{n'_k,v'_{y',k},z'_k\}\big)}{\partial n'_{k+1}},
\]
where $\eta(v'_{k+1})\equiv \frac{\partial \log(1-p(v'))}{\partial v'}\big|_{v'=v'_{k+1}}$ is the semi-elasticity of the job-finding probability with respect to the promised value.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
See Appendix~\ref{proof:wagegrowth}.
\end{proof}

This relationship captures the insurance–incentives trade-off. When the marginal value of a worker,
$E_{y'\mid y}\big[\partial J/\partial n'_{k+1}\big]$, is positive, the firm prefers to retain workers and backloads wages (raising $v'$). When the marginal value is negative, the firm lowers wages to encourage quits. This motivates a target wage:

\begin{definition}
The target wage for cohort $k$, $w^*_k\big(y,\{n_k,v_k,z_k\}/v_k\big)$, is the wage associated with the promised value $v^*_k\big(y,\{n_k,v_k,z_k\}/v_k\big)$ that solves
\[
M\big(y,\{n_k\},v_0,\ldots,v_k,\ldots,\{z_k\}\big)
\equiv E_{y'\mid y}\frac{\partial J\big(y',\{n'_k,v'_k,z'_k\}\big)}{\partial n'_{k+1}}=0,
\]
where $\{n'_k,v'_k,z'_k\}$ are next-period states implied by Lemma~\ref{firmproblem}. The target wage equals
\[
w^*_k\big(y,\{n_k,v_k,z_k\}/v_k\big)
= u'^{-1}\!\left(-\,\frac{n'_{k+1}}{\partial J\big(y',\{n'_k,v'_k,z'_k\}\big)/\partial v'_{y',k+1}}\right).
\]
\end{definition}

At the target, the cohort’s marginal profit is zero, making the firm indifferent to poaching (while still preferring to shed low-quality matches within the cohort). The target wage governs within-firm wage movements:

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:targetwage}
For any state $(y,\{n_k,v_k,z_k\})$ and cohort $k$ there exists a target wage $w^*_k$ such that:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Wages move toward the target:
\[
w_k \le w^*_k \;\Rightarrow\; w_k \le w'_{k+1} \le w^*_k,\qquad
w_k \ge w^*_k \;\Rightarrow\; w^*_k \le w'_{k+1} \le w_k.
\]
\item The farther from target, the faster the adjustment:
\[
|w_k-w^*_k|\ge |w_{k'}-w^*_{k'}| \;\Rightarrow\; |w'_{k+1}-w_k|\ge |w'_{k'+1}-w'_{k'}|.
\]
To first order:
\item $w^*_k$ increases in $z_k$ and is unchanged in $z_{k'}$ for $k'\neq k$.
\item All target wages respond equally to other state variables:
\[
\frac{\partial w^*_k}{\partial s}=\frac{\partial w^*_{k'}}{\partial s}\quad \forall k,k',\; s\neq z_k,z_{k'}.
\]
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
See Appendix~\ref{proof:targetwage}.
\end{proof}

Thus, at any state and for any cohort, wages adjust toward $w^*_k$. Layoffs are the firm’s only instrument to alter within-cohort quality; by item (4), they are also the only way to differentially move targets across cohorts.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:qualityconv}
Consider a state $(y,\{n_k,v_k\})$ and a cohort $k$ with $z_k<1$. Optimal layoffs satisfy
\[
-\,E_{y'\mid y}\frac{\partial J'}{\partial n'_{k+1}}\big(1-p(v'_{k+1})\big)
+ E_{y'\mid y}\frac{\partial J'}{\partial z'_{k+1}}\,\frac{\partial z'_{k+1}}{\partial s_k}\,\frac{1}{n_k}
- \frac{R(v'_{k+1})-U}{u'(w_k)} \;\le\; 0,
\]
with $s_k\ge 0$ and complementary slackness. Moreover, cohorts with lower promised values are more exposed to layoffs:
\[
v_k \le v_{k'} \;\Rightarrow\; s_k(1-z_k)n_k \;\ge\; s_{k'}(1-z_{k'})n_{k'} \quad \forall\,k'\le K,\; z_{k'}<1.
\]
To first order, layoff decisions load equally on quality across cohorts:
\[
\frac{\partial s_k}{\partial z_k}=\frac{\partial s_k}{\partial z_{k'}} \quad \forall\,k'\le K.
\]
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
See Appendix~\ref{proof:qualityconv}.
\end{proof}

As with wages, layoffs trade off the marginal value of a worker, $E_{y'\mid y}[\partial J'/\partial n'_{k+1}]$, against the compensation cost $\big(R(v'_{k+1})-U\big)/u'(w_k)$. The distinctive term is the quality effect,
$E_{y'\mid y}[\partial J'/\partial z'_{k+1}]\,(\partial z'_{k+1}/\partial s_k)/n_k$, since quality cannot be priced but can be \emph{selected} via layoffs. Lower-$v$ cohorts are more exposed to layoffs regardless of quality (provided $z<1$). Barring large quality gaps, these lower-$v$ cohorts also tend to be farther below their targets and, after layoffs, exhibit faster wage growth.

Finally, to map these results to tenure patterns, note that higher-tenure workers generally have higher promised values than juniors due to wage backloading (Proposition~\ref{prop:targetwage}, item 1). Juniors, being owed less, are therefore more exposed to layoffs than seniors and, via the upward shift in their target wages induced by layoffs, experience smaller wage cuts (if any) than seniors.
